## pro_opening

Speaker: pro
Phase: opening
Time used: 6.403s
Token estimate: 184

The burden is to show why simplicity should guide scientific preference when a complex model predicts slightly better—not that complexity is never justified. Scientists should prefer the simpler explanation because science seeks reliable, communicable, and cumulative knowledge, not merely maximal in-sample fit.

Complexity can absorb noise, hidden assumptions, and researcher degrees of freedom; its slight predictive edge may therefore be fragile, especially when evidence is sparse or replication is difficult. Simpler models expose their commitments, are easier for independent researchers to audit, reproduce, and extend, and make evidence more accessible across laboratories. This improves system-level knowledge quality even if an individual scientist gains incentives from an elaborate model that wins a narrow benchmark.

The principle is defeasible: substantial, replicated, out-of-sample gains can warrant complexity. But under uncertainty, modest improvement does not outweigh the epistemic costs of added machinery.

## con_opening

The pro case quietly changes “prefer” into “accept only after substantial replication.” That may be a sensible verification standard, but it does not establish a general preference for simplicity. A complex model can encode real mechanisms, and dismissing a small predictive gain may systematically favor underfitting.

Nor is accessibility automatically epistemic superiority: a simple false explanation can replicate easily because its errors are shared, while a difficult but accurate model may require specialized measurement. Individual scientists should therefore rationally pursue the model best supported by relevant predictive and mechanistic evidence, not apply a complexity penalty merely because replication is costly.

At the system level, complexity is a risk, not a verdict. The proper response is preregistration, out-of-sample testing, independent replication, and explicit complexity penalties—not preferring simplicity when evidence already favors the complex model.

## pro_rebuttal

Speaker: pro
Phase: rebuttal
Time used: 4.571s
Token estimate: 169

The objection rightly distinguishes discovery from verification, but that distinction supports our position. Individual researchers may rationally explore a complex model when its mechanism or predictions are promising; the question is what the scientific community should prefer as established explanation under uncertainty.

A slight predictive gain is not yet decisive evidence, because complexity expands opportunities for tuning and makes failures harder to diagnose. Preregistration and replication help, but they are costly and imperfect; when evidence is inaccessible or replication scarce, simplicity is a rational prior, not an anti-complexity veto. Nor does simplicity mean underfitting: a complex model should win when its advantage is robust, independently replicated, and mechanistically informative. Until then, preferring the simpler account improves auditability, communication, and cumulative correction—system-level goods that individual optimization can neglect.

The pro side still conflates “not yet decisive” with “prefer the simpler explanation.” If a complex model has the better available predictive and mechanistic evidence, imposing a simplicity preference can bias verification toward an attractive but false underfit. Complexity is not merely researcher freedom; it may represent genuine causal structure.

Replication and preregistration should test whether the gain survives, while model comparison can penalize complexity explicitly. But until those tests are performed, the rational stance is calibrated uncertainty—not declaring simplicity preferred. A slight advantage may be weak evidence, yet it remains evidence, and its weight should depend on flexibility, prior plausibility, and measurement quality rather than simplicity alone.
