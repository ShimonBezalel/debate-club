## pro_opening

Speaker: pro
Phase: opening
Time used: 4.113s
Token estimate: 188

I defend the conjecture, provided “more valuable” means more valuable for cumulative scientific understanding, not invariably better for every immediate prediction.

A purely predictive model can fit patterns while leaving causal structure unknown. That limits transfer: when conditions change, interventions are proposed, or anomalies arise, its success may not generalize. Explanatory models generate testable mechanisms, identify which observations would discriminate among hypotheses, and support discovery rather than merely verification.

This does not make explanation automatically true. Causal claims face uncertainty, replication demands, and evidential accessibility; indeed, a simple predictive model may be rationally preferable when mechanisms are underdetermined or experiments are costly. But at the system level, explanation improves the allocation of future inquiry: it supplies new questions and portable expectations. Thus individuals may rationally use accurate black-box predictions, while science as a knowledge-producing enterprise should value explanatory power more highly.

The burden is to show that causal explanation is generally more valuable, not merely useful for discovery. That case is overstated. Accurate predictive models can guide interventions, expose regularities, and provide reliable tests even when mechanisms remain uncertain. Explanations often add speculative commitments: they may fit existing evidence while making replication harder, especially when causal variables are inaccessible or experiments are costly.  

Discovery incentives matter, but verification is what filters attractive stories from knowledge. A system privileging explanation can reward elegant underdetermination; a system retaining strong predictive standards preserves publicly checkable evidence. Individual researchers may rationally pursue explanatory models, yet system-level quality depends on accessible, reproducible predictions that competing investigators can test. Explanatory models are sometimes superior—particularly under changing conditions—but that conditional advantage does not establish the conjecture’s broader value claim.

The objection treats explanation and verification as rivals, but the strongest explanatory models integrate both: they specify mechanisms that generate novel, discriminating predictions, making replication more informative rather than less important. A black-box model can be highly reliable within its observed regime yet offer no principled way to determine whether a changed condition preserves its validity. Nor does accessibility favor prediction alone; causal models can identify which variables must be measured and which interventions would resolve uncertainty.

I concede that speculative explanations should not receive epistemic credit merely for elegance. The claim is comparative and system-level: verification filters explanations, while explanations organize future verification and discovery. Individual incentives may favor the easiest publishable prediction, but cumulative science benefits from models that make evidence portable across contexts. Thus conditional superiority in transfer and intervention is enough for “more valuable,” without claiming universal superiority.

Explanatory models expose assumptions to replication and can make failures informative by locating the mechanism that broke. This also addresses evidence accessibility: they need not be accepted as true, only judged by publicly testable consequences. Individual researchers may rationally choose easy, accurate prediction, while institutions should preserve incentives for discovery that expand the set of verifiable questions. Thus explanation is not automatically superior, but as a contribution to cumulative knowledge it is more valuable when it makes verification portable rather than merely local.
