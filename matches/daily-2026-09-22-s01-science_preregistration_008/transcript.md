## pro_opening

Speaker: pro
Phase: opening
Time used: 4.480s
Token estimate: 180

Preregistration benefits exploratory research because it separates discovery from verification rather than suppressing discovery. Researchers can still explore unexpected patterns, revise hypotheses, and generate new questions; they simply label those analyses as exploratory instead of presenting them as predictions. That distinction improves incentives: individual researchers retain flexibility, while the scientific system gains clearer evidence and fewer opportunities to mistake post hoc stories for confirmed findings.

The relevant comparison is not flexibility versus rigidity, but undocumented flexibility versus transparent flexibility. Preregistration makes uncertainty visible, preserves a record of what was planned, and lets others assess whether results replicate under genuinely specified tests. It also makes evidence more accessible: null results and deviations are interpretable rather than silently disappearing. Although registration imposes time and may constrain opportunistic analysis, those costs are outweighed by more reliable cumulative knowledge.

## con_opening

Speaker: con
Phase: opening
Time used: 5.469s
Token estimate: 184

The burden is to show that preregistration’s reliability gains outweigh its costs specifically for exploration, not merely for confirmatory testing. Exploration depends on adaptive questioning: early observations can rationally redirect sampling, measures, and hypotheses. A fixed public plan can create paperwork, discourage low-resource investigators, and make researchers avoid promising but initially uncertain paths. Labeling everything “exploratory” preserves formal honesty, but may not preserve equal publication or funding incentives; discoveries remain individually risky while verification is rewarded later, if at all.

Transparency and replication matter, and preregistration can help distinguish prediction from hindsight. But that benefit can be obtained through timestamped analysis histories, registered reports, and open data without requiring premature commitments. Since exploratory evidence is inherently provisional, the system should prioritize accessible materials and independent replication, not impose a one-size-fits-all gate that may reduce discovery at its source.

## pro_rebuttal

Speaker: pro
Phase: rebuttal
Time used: 4.130s
Token estimate: 188

The objection conflates preregistration with a fixed, one-shot plan. A preregistration can specify an initial design while permitting documented amendments, adaptive sampling, and clearly labeled new analyses. Thus it need not suppress exploration; it prevents retrospective flexibility from masquerading as prediction.

The key comparison is not whether verification is rewarded enough individually, but whether the system can distinguish promising discovery from reliable evidence. Without that distinction, incentives favor compelling post hoc narratives, while nulls, deviations, and failed replications remain difficult to interpret or access. Timestamped histories and open data help, but they do not by themselves establish what was known before seeing outcomes; preregistration supplies that evidentiary baseline.

The policy need not be a universal gate: proportionate templates and exemptions can reduce burdens. But absent preregistration, uncertainty is systematically understated, making cumulative knowledge less reliable even when individual exploration becomes easier.

The burden remains unmet: showing that a useful evidentiary baseline outweighs exploration’s distinctive costs. Amendments preserve flexibility only if researchers can afford the documentation and if journals and funders treat amended work fairly—precisely the incentive problem. A preregistered label does not ensure exploratory discoveries receive equal attention, while it can make uncertain, iterative projects less attractive to pursue.

Preregistration is strongest for verification, where planned tests and replication are the point. For exploration, timestamped notebooks, versioned analyses, open data, and independent replication can reveal the sequence of decisions without demanding premature specification. These tools also improve accessibility and expose uncertainty. We concede preregistration can clarify whether a claim was predicted, but that benefit does not establish that mandatory or default preregistration improves system-level knowledge more than it reduces the discoveries available for later verification.

## pro_closing

Speaker: pro
Phase: closing
Time used: 3.891s
Token estimate: 187

The opposition has shifted from “preregistration” to mandatory, burdensome preregistration—a claim we need not defend. The proposition is comparative: exploratory research benefits more than it loses. A proportionate default with amendments, exemptions, and low-cost templates preserves adaptive inquiry while recording when decisions changed.

Their alternatives—notes, version histories, and open data—improve accessibility, but cannot reliably establish which hypotheses or stopping rules preceded outcomes. Replication is not a substitute for knowing whether an original result was prediction, discovery, or selective reporting; otherwise failed replication leaves the evidential status ambiguous.

Yes, individual researchers may face documentation costs, and discovery incentives need protection. But system-level knowledge quality depends on making provisional findings legible and verifiable, not merely maximizing each researcher’s short-term flexibility. Transparent exploration does not eliminate uncertainty; it prevents uncertainty from being misrepresented as confirmation. On balance, that is a net epistemic gain.

The core burden remains comparative: show net benefit for exploration, not merely improved verification. Preregistration can record prior intentions, but its gains are conditional on compliance, readable amendments, and fair treatment of deviations. In practice, those conditions impose costs precisely where exploration is most iterative and resources are scarcest.

The pro side concedes that discovery incentives need protection, yet offers no reason to expect journals or funders to value amended exploratory work equally. Thus individual rationality can shift toward safer, less adaptive projects, reducing the discoveries that verification later depends on. System-level knowledge loses at the source.

Preregistration clarifies evidential status, but timestamped notebooks, versioned analyses, open data, and replication can provide accessible uncertainty without premature commitments. We support voluntary or proportionate registration for claims approaching confirmation; as a broad default for exploration, its reliability gains do not outweigh its flexibility and discovery costs.
