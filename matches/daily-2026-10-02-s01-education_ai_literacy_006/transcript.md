## pro_opening

Speaker: pro
Phase: opening
Time used: 3.437s
Token estimate: 196

AI literacy should be a core subject—not because every student must become an AI engineer, but because students need durable skills to question, use, and verify AI across their lives. That means understanding basic concepts and limitations, checking outputs against evidence, protecting privacy, and recognizing when human judgment matters.

Teaching these skills systematically can improve learning outcomes: students become less likely to mistake fluent answers for reliable ones and more able to use tools productively. A quick boost in assignment performance is not enough; curricula should assess whether understanding transfers to unfamiliar tasks and persists over time.

A core requirement also promotes fairer access than leaving AI know-how to family resources or optional electives. Implementation must be age-appropriate and adapted by subject: evaluating AI in science differs from using it in writing. To limit teacher workload, integrate foundational lessons into existing courses and provide training and materials.

## con_opening

Speaker: con
Phase: opening
Time used: 3.509s
Token estimate: 184

AI literacy matters, but that does not establish that it should be a separate core subject alongside mathematics and language. The burden is to show that a universal requirement produces more durable learning than integrating relevant skills into existing subjects—without displacing foundational instruction or overloading teachers.

Students need practice judging AI outputs in context: evidence in science, authorship in writing, and privacy across subjects. A standalone course risks teaching tool-specific routines that boost short-term performance but fade as systems change. Integration can instead reinforce durable understanding where students apply it, while allowing age- and learner-appropriate depth.

Access is a real concern; schools should provide equitable instruction and resources. But equal access does not require equal course status. Given varied learners, subject needs, and limited teacher capacity, make AI literacy a supported cross-curricular responsibility, not a new core subject.

## pro_rebuttal

Speaker: pro
Phase: rebuttal
Time used: 2.832s
Token estimate: 179

The objection treats “core subject” as necessarily a separate course competing with foundational instruction. That is not the only model: core status can mean guaranteed, assessed learning outcomes taught through existing subjects, with dedicated time where needed. Integration without that guarantee risks making AI literacy uneven—the very access problem the opponent acknowledges.

Nor is contextual practice an argument against shared foundations. Students need both durable principles—verification, bias, privacy, limits—and subject-specific application. A standalone, tool-focused course could become obsolete; a core framework should teach transferable judgment and be revisited across ages and disciplines.

Teacher workload is a genuine constraint, not proof that the requirement is wrong. Provide training and ready-to-use materials, and assess transfer over time rather than short-term task performance. The case is for assured, adaptable instruction, not one identical course for every learner.
