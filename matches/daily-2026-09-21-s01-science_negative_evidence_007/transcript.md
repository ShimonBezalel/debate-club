## pro_opening

Speaker: pro
Phase: opening
Time used: 4.817s
Token estimate: 184

The burden is to show why, under ordinary scientific uncertainty, missing expected evidence should generally update belief more than an isolated surprise should. The reason is asymmetry: a well-specified prediction creates a search target, while surprising evidence is often vulnerable to noise, selection effects, measurement error, or undisclosed flexibility. Absence is not automatically disconfirmation—evidence may be inaccessible, replication underpowered, or the expectation poorly grounded—but when detectability and opportunity were adequate, non-observation directly penalizes the hypothesis.

This also serves system-level knowledge. Researchers have incentives to discover striking anomalies, whereas verification and replication are slower and less rewarding; weighting surprises too heavily therefore amplifies publication and selection bias. Individually, a surprising result may rationally prompt investigation, but provisionally it should move belief less than repeated failure to find an expected effect. Discovery incentives generate hypotheses; verification incentives determine which survive.

## con_opening

Speaker: con
Phase: opening
Time used: 3.151s
Token estimate: 185

The pro case quietly shifts from “absence usually counts more” to “absence under adequate detectability counts more,” which is a much narrower and more defensible claim. The missing condition is often the hard part: expected evidence may be inaccessible, delayed, weakly measured, or absent because the theory’s auxiliary assumptions failed. Conversely, surprising evidence can be independently replicated, mechanistically coherent, and produced by a method with high specificity. Its surprise is then precisely what makes it informative, not a reason to discount it.

Discovery incentives do create false positives, but verification incentives can also entrench consensus and underinvest in anomalies. At the individual level, rational updating must track likelihood ratios, not a blanket evidence hierarchy. At the system level, replication and accessibility matter symmetrically: repeated, transparent surprises may outweigh repeated non-observation when the latter had poor power or ambiguous observability.

The objection correctly identifies the qualification, but that qualification supports rather than defeats the conjecture: “usually” concerns ordinary cases where detectability is reasonably established, not inaccessible or poorly powered tests. Bayesian updating tracks likelihood ratios, yet expected evidence typically has a clearer counterfactual: if the hypothesis were true, observation should have been likely. A surprise becomes strong evidence only after independent replication, specificity, and mechanism are demonstrated—conditions rarely available at discovery. Thus an individual should investigate anomalies, but provisionally discount them relative to robust non-observation. Systemically, this distinction matters: discovery incentives already amplify striking results, while verification is underrewarded. Giving surprises equal initial weight worsens selection bias; requiring accessible, repeated evidence lets genuine anomalies survive without allowing one-off surprises to dominate knowledge.

“Usually” cannot smuggle in “ordinary cases” where detectability is already established; that is the disputed premise. Likelihood-ratio reasoning does not privilege absence: a single observation can be overwhelmingly unlikely under the incumbent theory, while many non-observations may be weak if power, measurement, or auxiliary assumptions are uncertain. Nor is replication a prerequisite for rational updating; it is a reason to calibrate confidence and seek verification. The incentive asymmetry cuts both ways: discovery incentives produce false positives, but consensus and funding incentives can suppress anomalies, making absence systematically easier to accumulate than accessible surprising evidence. At the individual level, one should weight evidence by diagnosticity, not by whether it is negative or surprising. At the system level, transparent anomalies should trigger replication rather than automatic discounting. Thus robust absence often matters greatly, but no general priority is established.

The conjecture does not claim a mechanical priority for every null result; it claims a default under ordinary uncertainty. The relevant comparison is diagnosticity, and expected evidence usually supplies the stronger benchmark: when observation was accessible, adequately powered, and specifically predicted, repeated non-observation lowers confidence directly. A surprise may be highly diagnostic, but at discovery it more often carries unresolved risks of selection, measurement, and flexibility; replication and mechanistic coherence can later reverse that balance. Thus rational individuals should investigate anomalies without treating one as decisive. Systemically, discovery incentives already reward novelty, while verification is underprovided; a presumption favoring reproducible failures protects knowledge quality without suppressing genuine anomalies. Where absence is inaccessible or weakly powered, it should count little—precisely why the qualified default, rather than an absolute rule, is defensible.

The qualification does the real work, but it does not establish the conjecture’s direction. “Accessible,” “adequately powered,” and “specifically predicted” are themselves uncertain, and repeated nulls can inherit the same flawed auxiliary assumptions. Meanwhile, a surprising observation may be independently replicated, highly specific, and mechanistically linked before any consensus forms; rational updating should reflect its likelihood ratio, not its novelty. The defensible rule is not “absence usually outweighs surprise,” but “weight evidence by diagnosticity, accessibility, and replication.” That supports cautious anomalies and strong nulls without granting either a default priority.
