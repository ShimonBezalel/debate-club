## pro_opening

Speaker: pro
Phase: opening
Time used: 4.878s
Token estimate: 195

A fixed share of research funding should be reserved for replication studies because discovery and verification serve different incentives. Individual researchers may rationally prefer novel results: they bring visibility, grants, and careers. But science is a system, and knowledge quality depends on whether important findings survive independent testing. Without dedicated funding, replication is systematically underprovided, especially when results are ambiguous, inconvenient, or commercially unglamorous.

The reservation should be a floor, not an inflexible command: its percentage can vary by field, risk, and the existing evidence base. Replications should be methodologically independent, preregistered where appropriate, and their data and materials made accessible. This does not imply every study deserves repetition or that replication automatically establishes truth. It means uncertainty is measured rather than hidden, correcting false positives and clarifying which claims merit further investment. Discovery funding remains intact; the policy protects the reliability of what discovery produces.

The burden is not to show that replication is valuable, but that a fixed funding reservation is superior to flexible, field-sensitive allocation. We oppose the mandate. Discovery and verification have different incentives, but that does not justify one universal quota: some fields need extensive replication, while others face rapidly changing methods, scarce samples, or inherently nonrepeatable phenomena. A fixed share can displace promising discovery or fund low-value repetitions merely to satisfy a target.

We concede that replication is systematically underprovided and that accessible data, preregistration, and independent checks improve evidence. But those goals can be pursued through targeted grants, replication requirements for high-impact claims, and funding agencies’ periodic review—not a rigid reservation. System-level knowledge quality matters, yet it depends on prioritizing the most decision-relevant uncertainties, not treating all research budgets alike.

## pro_rebuttal

Speaker: pro
Phase: rebuttal
Time used: 4.912s
Token estimate: 163

The objection treats “fixed” as “universal and rigid,” which is not my proposal. A fixed share can be a field-sensitive floor: agencies set different percentages, review them periodically, and permit justified exceptions for nonrepeatable phenomena or scarce samples. Without a protected floor, however, flexible allocation predictably favors visible discovery over less prestigious verification; targeted grants alone leave many important but unprofitable findings inaccessible to independent testing. The relevant comparison is not replication versus discovery, but marginal discovery funding versus the knowledge gained by resolving uncertainty in existing claims. Replication need not mean mechanical repetition: preregistration, shared materials, and independent designs can test robustness and expose boundary conditions. A reservation therefore preserves system-level evidence quality while retaining discretion about which claims merit replication.

## con_rebuttal

Speaker: con

Time used: 3.750s

The rebuttal concedes the central problem: replication is underprovided because discovery incentives dominate individually rational choices. But a “field-sensitive floor” remains a reservation in substance, and the key question is whether a preset share improves decisions over prioritizing claims by importance, uncertainty, and feasibility. It may not. A quota can consume scarce funds on accessible, repeatable studies while neglecting nonrepeatable phenomena, new methods, or genuinely high-value discovery. Exceptions and periodic review merely reintroduce flexible allocation—without proving the quota adds value.

Replication also does not automatically resolve uncertainty: failures may reflect changed populations, measurement, or implementation, while inaccessible data and materials can block interpretation. Fund independent checks where expected information gain is high, require transparency for consequential claims, and condition further funding on evidential strength. That targets verification without sacrificing discovery by arithmetic.

## pro_closing

Speaker: pro
Phase: closing
Time used: 2.862s
Token estimate: 173

The opposition identifies real design risks, but not a reason to abandon a protected floor. “Prioritize by information gain” sounds superior only if agencies can reliably identify that gain beforehand; uncertainty about which claims matter is precisely why a portfolio needs verification capacity. A reservation need not fund indiscriminate repetition: competitive review can select consequential, uncertain, feasible tests, while exceptions protect nonrepeatable work. Nor does a failed replication mechanically falsify a claim; independent designs, preregistration, and accessible data help distinguish genuine instability from changed populations or methods. The policy addresses a system-level market failure: discovery earns private career rewards, whereas verification benefits the entire research community. Flexible programs may help, but without a guaranteed minimum they remain vulnerable to discovery’s stronger incentives. Thus a reviewable, field-sensitive floor complements—not replaces—discovery funding.

The burden remains unmet: show that reserving a preset share improves knowledge more than selecting verification by expected information gain. A floor may protect replication, but it also creates arithmetic allocation: money is spent because the quota exists, while high-value discovery or unusual, nonrepeatable work loses resources. Exceptions and field-specific percentages do not solve this; they make the policy a flexible prioritization system, while adding bureaucratic rigidity where flexibility is needed.

We concede that individual incentives favor discovery and that system-level knowledge needs independent checks, transparency, and accessible evidence. But those goals support targeted replication grants, preregistration, data-sharing requirements, and funding conditions for consequential claims—not a fixed reservation. Replication is valuable, yet its value depends on claim importance, uncertainty, feasibility, and interpretability. Fund those checks aggressively; do not guarantee them a percentage by rule.
