## pro_opening

Speaker: pro
Phase: opening
Time used: 4.749s
Token estimate: 188

Schools should prefer open educational resources as their default—not require them in every case. “Prefer” means choosing an OER when it meets the course’s learning needs, while allowing a commercial text when evidence or classroom fit justifies the cost.

OER can widen access by letting every student use the material without purchase barriers, and teachers can adapt examples, reading levels, or local context. That flexibility may support motivation and durable understanding, not merely short-term test performance. But adaptation takes time, and a polished commercial text may better serve a subject or learner group; schools should account for teacher workload and provide support.

The standard should be demonstrated learning over time, alongside access and usability—not price or novelty alone. Because needs differ across learners and subjects, schools should review outcomes and retain the option to choose commercial materials when they work better.

## con_opening

Speaker: con
Phase: opening
Time used: 4.927s
Token estimate: 171

The burden is not to show that commercial textbooks are always better; it is to show why schools should not prefer OER as a default. Learning materials must work for particular students, subjects, and teachers. An OER’s adaptability and free access can help, but neither guarantees accurate, coherent instruction or durable understanding. A polished commercial text may better sequence difficult concepts, provide practice, and reduce preparation time—especially where teachers lack capacity to evaluate or adapt materials.

Nor should short-term test gains settle the question: schools should consider lasting understanding, motivation, and access. Those outcomes may favor OER for some learners, but others benefit from structured, consistent materials. The prudent policy is to choose the resource that best serves the course, not to privilege one licensing model in advance.

## pro_rebuttal

The opposition’s “choose what works best” sounds neutral, but it leaves access barriers and textbook cost out of the default decision. A school can prefer OER without assuming every OER is accurate or coherent: require review for quality and fit, and choose a commercial text when it better supports that course.

Nor does a polished text automatically reduce teacher workload; OER may require adaptation, while commercial materials may still need supplementation. Schools should compare total workload and learning over time—not just short-term test scores—and check motivation and durable understanding across different learners and subjects. That is a conditional preference, not a licensing rule: use OER when it meets the need, and retain commercial options when it does not.

The pro side has narrowed “prefer” to “choose OER when it works,” but that is simply a quality-first policy—not a reason to privilege OER. Reviewing and adapting OER also consumes teacher time. That burden may be manageable in some subjects, but not where teachers lack capacity or learners need carefully sequenced instruction. Commercial texts are not automatically superior, and cost barriers should be addressed. But schools should compare access, workload, and learning outcomes for their actual students, rather than make licensing status the default.
